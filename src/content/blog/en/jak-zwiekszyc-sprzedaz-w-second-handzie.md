How can a second-hand shop increase sales? The first answer is often to attract more customers. Advertising matters, but it is not the only route to growth. If a shop already receives steady footfall, extra turnover may come from better stock, a smarter product mix, helpful staff and a larger average basket.

The basic model is simple:

**number of transactions × average transaction value = turnover.**

The result therefore depends not only on how many people enter the shop, but also on how many buy, how many items they choose and how much each transaction is worth. Below are four practical ways to improve second-hand shop turnover and average basket value.

## Start by finding the real sales constraint

For two to four weeks, record visitor numbers, transactions, items sold, turnover and average transaction value. This helps distinguish a traffic problem from low conversion or a basket that is too small.

- High footfall and few transactions may point to unsuitable stock, pricing or merchandising.
- A healthy number of transactions with a low average value suggests room for accessories, bundles and helpful cross-selling.
- Low footfall calls for better local visibility and promotion.

Without this basic measurement, a shop can spend more on advertising while its largest opportunity is already on the sales floor.

## 1. Do not buy stock simply because it is cheap

The lowest purchase price per kilogram does not always deliver the best commercial result. What matters is how much of a batch fits the shop’s audience, how quickly it can sell and what impression it creates on the rails.

Weak stock may occupy space for too long, require heavy discounts and reduce the perceived quality of the whole shop. A well-selected batch can help a retailer:

- set prices that reflect the quality of each item;
- increase the value of a transaction;
- create a stronger, more coherent display;
- give customers a reason to return regularly.

When comparing batches, consider grade, indicative composition, season, the share of categories your customers want and the potential share of unsaleable pieces—not only the headline price. Estimate the possible revenue from the complete batch rather than focusing on the percentage markup of one very cheap item.

## 2. Add complementary product categories

A customer who has chosen trousers or a jumper may also need a belt, bag, scarf or pair of shoes. Complementary categories make a multi-item purchase more likely when they suit the audience and are easy to notice.

Test a small quantity of categories such as:

- bags, wallets, belts and scarves;
- jewellery and small accessories;
- seasonal footwear;
- toys or children’s items;
- ready-made clothing combinations by style or colour.

The goal is not to fill the shop at random. Track the sell-through of each new category. Display accessories next to relevant clothing, on mannequins and in a tidy checkout area. Compare [wholesale clothing mixes](/en/wholesale-assortment/clothing-mixes/) and [second-hand footwear](/en/wholesale-assortment/footwear/) when planning complementary lines.

## 3. Train staff to cross-sell helpfully

Cross-selling should not feel pushy. A useful suggestion saves a customer time and points out something they may have missed. Someone choosing trousers can be shown suitable jumpers or belts; a customer buying children’s clothing can be directed to matching sizes and recent arrivals.

Simply telling staff to “sell more” is rarely enough. Build a small, repeatable system:

1. Prepare a few natural phrases for the most common purchases.
2. Make sure the team knows where complementary products are displayed.
3. Suggest them after understanding the customer’s need, not immediately at the entrance.
4. Review average transaction value and items per transaction each week.
5. Discuss which recommendations genuinely helped customers.

Consider an illustrative example: 100 transactions at an average of PLN 40 produce PLN 4,000 of daily turnover. Raising the average to PLN 45 adds PLN 500 in this model. This is not a forecast; the real outcome depends on margin, costs, stock quality and customer behaviour.

## 4. Use promotions that grow the basket

A promotion does not have to discount everything. Its purpose can be to encourage customers to reach a basket threshold or add another item.

Mechanics worth testing include:

- a discount on the next item after reaching a set spend;
- an extra discount on the fourth, lower-priced item;
- a bundle from one product category;
- a small free item after reaching a clear threshold.

Calculate the campaign before launch: define its cost, minimum basket, expected gross margin and duration. Keep the rules simple and display them at the entrance, on the sales floor and at checkout. Staff can remind customers, but the conditions should never be a surprise at payment.

## Metrics to review every week

A small dashboard with six figures is enough to evaluate progress:

- visitor count;
- transaction count;
- visitor-to-buyer conversion rate;
- average transaction value;
- average items per transaction;
- turnover and gross margin.

Test one meaningful change at a time. You will then be better able to tell whether the result came from a new batch, accessory merchandising, the sales team or a promotion.

## A 30-day sales improvement plan

**Week 1:** record the baseline and identify the strongest and weakest product categories.

**Week 2:** improve merchandising and add one complementary product group.

**Week 3:** run a short staff session on helpful cross-selling and begin measuring items per basket.

**Week 4:** launch one carefully costed basket-building promotion, then compare the results with week one.
